SIERRA NEVADA TRIP REPORT

Four Days on the Sierra High Route, and What the Snow Bridge Taught Me

A cross-country line above 11,000 feet, a June snowpack that hadn't read the forecast, and the one decision that mattered more than any piece of gear I carried.

Mile 0 — The approach

We started from Piute Pass trailhead at 5:40am, headlamps still on, the kind of cold that makes you double-check your zipper is all the way up. The plan was straightforward on paper: four days, three passes, one exit at Bishop Pass. Paper doesn't account for snowpack, and this was a 118%-of-normal year — the ranger at the permit kiosk had said as much the day before, in the tone people use when they're being polite about telling you your plan might not survive contact with reality.

By mile six the trail had mostly given up being a trail. We were reading the terrain instead — the line of least resistance between talus fields, the places where the granite held less snow, the faint suncups that meant someone else had post-holed through a week earlier and given up a little less snow for us to deal with.

Piute Creek was running loud and full by 9am, which told us more about the day ahead than any forecast would have. Snowmelt rivers peak in the afternoon; if a crossing looks marginal at nine, it will not look better at two. We filed that away and kept moving.

Mile 14 — The snow bridge

The crossing below Feather Pass had one obvious line: a snow bridge over the creek, maybe four feet wide, with an audible rush of water underneath. We probed it with a trekking pole first, then a boot on the edge, and didn't love the sound — a hollow, shifting note instead of a solid one. We backed off and spent forty minutes finding a longer but solid route instead, a rock hop about two hundred yards upstream where the creek split into three narrower channels.

That forty minutes is the whole trip report, honestly. Everything else is just walking. It's also the decision I keep coming back to when people ask what the "hardest part" of a trip like this actually is — it's rarely the elevation. It's the fifteen seconds of deciding whether to trust something you can't fully see under.

Two hikers coming the other direction told us the bridge had held for them that morning. It's a reminder that trail conditions are a snapshot, not a guarantee — the same crossing can be sound at 7am and rotten by 10.

Mile 22 — Muriel Col, at last light

We hit Muriel Col later than planned, the sun already low enough to turn the granite orange. The descent off the north side was a boot-ski down consolidated spring snow — the good kind, forgiving underfoot, nothing like the breakable crust we'd fought through at mile nine. We made camp at a tarn just below 11,000 feet, water still half-frozen at the edges, and ate dinner watching the light go from orange to violet over the ridge we'd just come off.

This is the part that doesn't photograph well and matters most: the twenty minutes after camp is set, before it's fully dark, when the day's decisions finally stop needing to be made.

Mile 26 — What I'd carry again

  • Microspikes — used them twice, both times non-negotiable, both times on north-facing snow that had refrozen overnight.
  • A second map layer with recent trip reports, not just the topo — the snow bridge would not have surprised us if we'd read the last week's reports more carefully.
  • One fewer freeze-dried dinner and one more real one for night two, when appetite actually shows up after a big pass day.
  • A lighter puffy than I brought — the tarn camp at 11,000 feet never dropped below what a 20°F bag and a mid-weight layer could handle.

Mile 38 — The exit

We came off at Bishop Pass a half-day early, legs fine, judgment intact. That felt like the actual summit — not the highest point on the map, but the moment where every call along the way turned out to have been the right one, including the ones that cost us time.

Next season I want to try the same route a month later, once the creek crossings have dropped and the snow bridges have stopped being a question at all. There's a version of this trip that's a straightforward walk, and I'm curious what it teaches instead.

JR

About

Waypoint is one person's trail journal, kept mostly honest.

I'm Jules — I started Waypoint after a thru-hike where the only writing I did was two lines a night in a shredded notebook. This is that notebook, typed up and made public.

No sponsorships, no gear-review quotas. If something's in a dispatch, it's because it earned its place in the pack — or because it failed spectacularly and there's a lesson in that.

Most entries come from the Sierra Nevada, where I live an hour from three trailheads, with occasional dispatches from further afield when a trip warrants it. I work a normal job that has nothing to do with the outdoors, which is part of why the writing happens in short, honest bursts instead of polished monthly features.

Every dispatch follows the same rule: it gets written the same day the trip ends, or as close to it as sleep allows, before the hard parts get sanded down into a better story than what actually happened.

A few questions people ask

How often does the dispatch actually go out?

Roughly once a month, tied to when a trip report is actually ready — not on a fixed content calendar. Some months bring two; occasionally a month brings none.

Is this a gear-affiliate newsletter in disguise?

No. Nothing on Waypoint is sponsored, and there are no affiliate links in dispatches. Gear gets mentioned only when it earned a place in the pack on a real trip.

Can I unsubscribe easily?

One click, no trailhead required, no "are you sure" flow designed to talk you out of it.
